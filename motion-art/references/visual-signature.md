# Visual signature: what the renders look like

The other references explain the *method*. This one is about the *look*, distilled from
viewing all 534 thumbnails and several full-size images. **Before you write code, open 2–3
images in `assets/refs/`** that are closest to your subject. Use `Read` on the JPG. Compare
your render to them before you call it done.

## The rules the renders obey
1. **Every surface shows marks.** There are no smooth gradients and no clean vector fills
   anywhere. Skies are made of dozens or hundreds of horizontal brush streaks (`line` with a
   soft custom brush, colour stepped band by band), or wet-in-wet `fill` bands with bleed.
   Flat areas carry grain lines, spray, or hatch. If a region of 200×200 px has no visible
   stroke, it is unfinished.
2. **Detail density belongs to the hero.** The hero object takes 35–70% of the frame and
   holds most of the strokes: rims, seams, labels, glints, droplets, fibres. The setting is
   simplified but still textured.
3. **Crisp against loose.** The most common contrast is a hard-edged, carefully lit object
   over a loose, bleeding, stained ground (scooter, sunglasses, pocket square). Finish the hero
   tightly and leave the ground loose.
4. **Fine line networks.** Wires, spokes, veins, roots, stitches, threads and caustics are
   drawn as many hairlines (`rotring`, `2H`, a fine custom liner). Thin linear detail is the
   most recognisable signature at thumbnail size.
5. **Shading in steps or glazes.** Use cel steps (2–4 flat value bands,
   as in the vending machine and the bicycle) or stacked translucent glazes (watercolour pieces).
6. **Grounds.** About one third are dark grounds (black, navy, lacquer) and about one third
   are cream paper. The rest are coloured or full-scene. Dark grounds still carry faint
   streaks or vignettes.
7. **Composition.** Most pieces show a single isolated hero. Next most common are
   symmetric or framed layouts (ornamental borders, mandalas, specimen grids, diagram
   plates), and only a minority are full scenes. In full scenes, keep the horizon low or high,
   never centred, and make the hero big and close.
   **Landmark or scene requests** (a vase with the Tower of Pisa behind it): the hero stays the
   near object, big and crisp. The landmark is a secondary layer: loose wet-in-wet, low
   detail, partly dissolved. Even full scenes leave bare cream paper at the edges and in
   blooms. A full-bleed, evenly finished background is what makes a render read as a poster.
8. **Annotations.** Small archival marks are frequent: registration crosses, ruler ticks,
   swatch strips, tiny labels, plate numbers, signatures. Big poster titles are rare. Use them
   only when the tradition demands them, and even then make the type hand-made, not clean.
9. **Plants and flowers** (beetroot, kale, marigold, chamomile, chintz). Leaves are glaze
   shapes with hatched or pen veins. Petals have layered translucent washes with darker edges
   and white-paper highlights. Stems are tapered strokes with pressure, not uniform lines.
   Bunches overlap and vary in angle, scale and colour.

## Self-critique before you finish
Run it on every render. Each item passes, or goes into the report as a known weakness.
- **Thumbnail:** resized to 200 px, it reads like the contact sheets, as a hand-made painting.
- **Marks:** every 200×200 px region shows strokes; edges carry bleed, texture and pressure.
- **Hero:** big (35–70% of the frame) and clearly the most detailed thing, with its specific
  hotspots legible up close (logos, seams, drops, fibres).
- **Contrast:** a crisp hero over a loose ground.
- **Thin elements:** stems, wires and edges are tapered and textured.
- **Light:** one direction for shadows, speculars and reflections.
- **Tradition:** recognisable without reading the brief.
- **Colour:** flat RGB appears only where the tradition calls for it (riso, marker).
